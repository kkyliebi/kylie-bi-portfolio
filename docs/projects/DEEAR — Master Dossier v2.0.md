## 01. Project Definition

### One-sentence positioning

**DEEAR is a collaborative editorial publication that used invitation, interview, sequencing, and visual pacing to create a psychologically safer environment for sharing personal experiences.**

### Project abstract

DEEAR began before the magazine existed.

The project depended on people being willing to disclose experiences that could be personal, emotional, socially sensitive, or difficult to summarise through a conventional interview. The first communication problem was therefore not how to lay out collected material. It was how to approach potential contributors in a way that established credibility, reduced judgement, and gave them meaningful control over how they participated.

Kylie's invitation strategy addressed that problem directly. It explained the project, made space for anonymous participation, and allowed contributions to take different forms, including words, photographs, songs, objects, and other personal references. This widened the interview beyond a fixed questionnaire and recognised that people do not all narrate experience in the same medium.

The material gathered by the team was then developed into a publication with the intimacy of a personal diary. Typography, spacing, image-text relationships, sequencing, and blank pages were used to shape rhythm rather than simply contain content. The publication moved between individual voices without forcing them into a single tone. Its editorial system gave the contributions proximity while preserving difference.

The final outcome was a collaborative magazine. Kylie's documented contribution includes the personal invitation and participation approach, expansion of the interview network, editorial layout development, typographic hierarchy, visual pacing, material integration, and support for narrative consistency across the publication. The finished magazine belongs to the team; individual decisions and contributions are identified where evidence supports them.

## 02. The Historical Beginning: A Communication Problem

### Original Intent

DEEAR required authentic personal material. That requirement created a problem upstream of editorial design:

> How can people be invited to share personal experiences without making disclosure feel extractive, prescriptive, or unsafe?

A standard request for an interview would have treated participation as a simple transaction: the project asks questions, the participant supplies answers, and the design team converts those answers into content. That model was insufficient for a publication concerned with subjective experience.

Personal narratives do not appear merely because a question has been asked. They depend on context:

- who is asking;
- why the material is being collected;
- how it may be used;
- whether the contributor can remain anonymous;
- whether the available response format fits the experience;
- whether the contributor expects judgement;
- and whether the publication appears capable of handling the material responsibly.

The project therefore began with the design of a relationship between the publication and its potential contributors. Before DEEAR could organise reading, it had to earn participation.

### Design consequence

The communication sequence did not begin with:

> content → layout → magazine

It began with:

> people → trust → permission → expression → collection → editorial interpretation → publication

This sequence is the central historical correction introduced in v2.0. It does not diminish the role of editorial design. It clarifies what the editorial system was built to hold.

## 03. Trust Building as a Design Decision

### Original Intent

Trust was not an abstract theme added to the project later. It was constructed through practical choices in the invitation and interview process.

The invitation had to do more than announce a magazine. It needed to answer the unspoken concerns of a potential contributor:

- Is this real?
- Who is behind it?
- Why are they asking me?
- Must I identify myself?
- What kind of answer is expected?
- Can I communicate indirectly?
- Will my experience be judged or simplified?

The invitation established a credible entry point into the project. It framed participation as open rather than compulsory and allowed contributors to determine the form and degree of disclosure.

### Evidence-backed trust mechanisms

#### 1. A clear invitation

The invitation made the request legible. It gave potential contributors enough context to decide whether participation felt appropriate.

#### 2. The option of anonymity

Anonymity reduced the social risk attached to disclosure. It separated the value of a contribution from the public identification of its author.

#### 3. Multiple forms of expression

Participants were not limited to formal written testimony. The invitation accepted different kinds of material, including objects, songs, photographs, and other references.

This was not decorative variety. It recognised that memory and identity are often carried through associations that are difficult to reproduce as direct statements.

#### 4. An open-ended response structure

The invitation did not demand one correct narrative form. It allowed contributors to choose how closely, indirectly, briefly, or expansively they responded.

#### 5. A non-judgemental tone

The project presentation describes a diary-like and judgement-free environment. The tone of the collection method and the tone of the publication therefore supported the same objective: contributors should not feel that their experience had to be defended before it could be included.

### What the invitation changed

The invitation expanded the project's interview network by making participation possible beyond an immediate circle. More importantly, it changed the quality of the request. Strangers were not treated as content sources; they were approached as people deciding whether to enter a communication environment.

This is a documented individual contribution by Kylie and should not be reduced to “supporting research” or absorbed into an undifferentiated team process.

## 04. Invitation Strategy

### Original Intent

The invitation was a working communication artefact. Its function was to create the conditions for the rest of the project.

It performed four connected tasks:

1. **Legitimise the request** by explaining the project and its purpose.
2. **Reduce participation risk** through anonymity and flexible disclosure.
3. **Expand the vocabulary of response** beyond conventional interview answers.
4. **Convert potential readers into contributors** without assuming prior familiarity or trust.

### Why a conventional interview invitation was insufficient

A conventional interview often centres the interviewer's information needs. DEEAR needed a different balance. The project needed material, but contributors needed agency over how they entered the conversation.

The invitation therefore operated as a threshold rather than a questionnaire. It did not only ask for information; it described an environment and allowed the recipient to decide how to cross into it.

### Communication decision

The key decision was to design for permission before extraction.

That decision later became visible in the magazine's editorial character. A publication that asks people to contribute carefully must also present their contributions carefully. The invitation and the final editorial pacing belong to the same communication logic, even though they appear at different stages of the project.

## 05. Interview and Collection Methodology

### Original Intent

The collection method was structured to receive different forms of personal expression. It combined interview material with images, objects, music, memories, and other associative evidence.

The method was not designed to produce standardised answers. Its purpose was to gather material that could preserve the specificity of each participant's experience while still becoming part of a shared publication.

### Method principles

#### Participant agency

Contributors could control what they shared and how directly they shared it.

#### Plural media

The project recognised that a photograph, object, or song can communicate an aspect of experience that a direct verbal explanation may flatten.

#### Open interpretation

Collected material was not valuable only when it produced a definitive statement. Ambiguity, association, and emotional resonance could also carry meaning.

#### Editorial responsibility

Once material was received, the team had to decide how to place it without overstating, sensationalising, or making different contributors appear interchangeable.

### From collection to editorial material

The collected contributions entered a process of selection, grouping, sequencing, and visual translation. This transition required judgement. The team was not merely assembling submissions; it was building a reading structure capable of holding multiple voices.

The process can be represented as:

> invitation → participation → interview and collection → interpretation → editorial grouping → sequencing → publication

This is not a neutral pipeline. Every step changes the context in which a contribution will be understood.

## 06. Concept Development

### Original Intent

DEEAR developed as an intimate publication rather than a detached report. The personal diary became an important conceptual reference because it offered an appropriate relationship between private expression and shared reading.

A diary carries several qualities relevant to the project:

- it can accommodate fragments;
- it does not require every entry to resolve;
- tone can shift from page to page;
- images and words can coexist without a fixed hierarchy;
- pauses are meaningful;
- and intimacy can be created through accumulation rather than explanation.

The publication adopted these qualities without pretending to be the private diary of one person. It became a collective editorial space in which different experiences could remain recognisably distinct.

### Conceptual proposition

DEEAR did not attempt to create one representative story.

It developed a framework in which personal narratives could be encountered beside one another, connected through editorial rhythm while resisting forced equivalence.

### From theme to system

The project contained themes of identity, migration, emotional memory, and queer experience. The editorial challenge was not simply to illustrate those subjects. It was to design:

- how a reader enters a contribution;
- how long they remain with it;
- what precedes and follows it;
- when visual material leads;
- when text needs space;
- where interruption is useful;
- and when an empty page should protect a transition.

The concept therefore became operational through the publication system.

## 07. Editorial System

### Original Intent

The editorial system translated a collection of heterogeneous contributions into a coherent reading experience. Coherence did not mean visual uniformity or narrative sameness. It meant establishing enough shared structure for the reader to move through the publication without erasing the character of individual material.

### System components

#### Typographic hierarchy

Typography established distinctions between voices, explanatory material, titles, transitions, and supporting information. Hierarchy helped the reader understand how to approach a page before resolving every detail of its content.

#### Layout rhythm

Page density varied across the publication. Dense passages were balanced by quieter spreads so that emotionally or visually complex material was not presented as a continuous block.

#### Image-text relationships

Images were not treated as decoration attached to written testimony. Their scale, cropping, position, and proximity to text shaped how associations formed.

#### Sequencing

The order of contributions produced meaning across pages. A spread could change the interpretation of the material before or after it. Sequencing therefore operated as narrative construction, not file arrangement.

#### Blank pages and negative space

Blank pages gave readers room to pause and allowed one contribution to end before another began. They functioned as editorial punctuation and as a form of care.

#### Repetition and variation

Recurring visual behaviours helped the magazine feel whole, while controlled variation prevented the system from suppressing the individuality of each contribution.

### The publication as environment

DEEAR v1.0 described the publication as a communication system. V2.0 makes the underlying relationship more precise:

> **The publication became the medium through which a communication environment could exist.**

The system mattered because it shaped the conditions under which contributors, material, and readers encountered one another.

## 08. Reading Experience

### Original Intent

The publication was designed to be read with attention rather than consumed as a rapid sequence of information. Its pacing moved between disclosure and distance, concentration and pause.

The diary-like approach supported an internal, reflective tone. Readers were not positioned as spectators receiving a resolved argument. They were invited to enter a sequence of partial perspectives.

### Reader relationship

The reader's role was not to verify whether an experience was sufficiently representative. The reader was asked to remain with difference.

Editorial decisions supported this relationship:

- open pages reduced visual pressure;
- shifts in scale marked changes in voice or emphasis;
- images introduced indirect forms of knowledge;
- blank pages slowed transition;
- and sequencing allowed resonance to build between contributions.

The reading experience therefore extended the project's trust logic. Contributors were given choices in how they expressed themselves; readers were given time and space in which to receive that expression.

## 09. Visual Language

### Original Intent

The visual language supported intimacy, plurality, and emotional pacing. It was contemporary without turning the contributions into a style exercise.

Its key qualities included:

- editorial restraint;
- clear typographic hierarchy;
- controlled shifts in scale;
- generous negative space;
- varied image-text composition;
- diary-like proximity;
- and a consistent publication rhythm across different content types.

The visual system did not attempt to make every story look the same. Its purpose was to establish continuity around heterogeneous material.

### Visual criteria

A visual decision was successful when it:

1. clarified how a contribution should be entered;
2. preserved the emotional register of the material;
3. strengthened relationships between pages;
4. supported the reader's orientation;
5. or created necessary space between different voices.

These criteria are more accurate than describing the work only through typography, layout, or aesthetic consistency. The design value lies in what those formal decisions enabled.

## 14. Looking Back: What the Project Revealed

### Later reflection

Looking back, DEEAR marks an early point at which Kylie's practice moved beyond treating communication as the design of an output.

The magazine mattered, but the decisive act happened earlier: people needed conditions in which expression felt possible.

This led to a broader understanding:

> **Before people are willing to communicate, they need a context in which communication feels psychologically safer.**

This is not a claim that editorial design can guarantee safety. Nor does it mean that an invitation removes every power imbalance involved in collecting personal material. It means that communication design can acknowledge those conditions and make deliberate choices about permission, anonymity, format, tone, and control.

### Trust as an operational concern

The later term **trust design** is useful when applied carefully. In DEEAR, it refers to the practical construction of credibility and participant agency through:

- transparent framing;
- voluntary participation;
- anonymity;
- flexible response formats;
- non-judgemental language;
- and responsible editorial handling.

The term is a later interpretation of documented decisions, not proof that the team formally named a “trust design methodology” during the project.

### Editorial design as relationship

DEEAR also changed how Kylie understood editorial work.

Editorial design is not only the arrangement of text and image. It organises relationships:

- contributor and project;
- testimony and context;
- image and language;
- one voice and another;
- disclosure and pause;
- publication and reader.

The publication's quality therefore depends on what those relationships allow, not only on the consistency of its graphic system.

## 15. Methodological Significance

### Discover

The project identified that the initial obstacle was not a shortage of visual ideas. It was the difficulty of gathering personal material responsibly and meaningfully.

### Connect

It connected invitation, anonymity, interviews, associative objects, songs, photographs, and editorial structure into one participation-to-publication process.

### Translate

It translated heterogeneous personal contributions into layouts, sequences, image-text relationships, and a shared reading rhythm.

### Materialise

It materialised that process as a collaborative magazine.

This four-part reading is a later portfolio methodology used to understand the project. It should not be presented as a formal sequence the team explicitly followed at the time unless further evidence confirms that terminology.

### Contribution to Kylie's practice

DEEAR provides evidence for a stable working pattern:

1. Kylie looks for the context that makes a communication problem possible.
2. She attends to relationships before isolating outputs.
3. She widens the available language of participation.
4. She translates complex or subjective material into a structured experience.
5. She uses form to support meaning rather than treating form as the project's starting point.
