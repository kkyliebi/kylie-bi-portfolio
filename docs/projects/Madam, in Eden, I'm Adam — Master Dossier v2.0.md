## 00. Purpose and Authority

This dossier is the authoritative working reference for **Madam, in Eden, I'm Adam**. It reconstructs the project from its original brief, research, narrative development, parallel storyboard explorations, collaborative production, final photographic book, and later reflection.

It is intended to support:

- portfolio and website writing;
- project presentations and case-study decks;
- interview preparation;
- future editorial reconstruction of the photographic book;
- image, research, and process-asset selection;
- accurate crediting of individual and collaborative contributions.

Shorter public texts should be derived from this document. They should not introduce new claims about authorship, research, outcomes, or project history that are not supported here or by the primary project archive.

### Editorial principle

This project should not be reduced to a photography exercise or presented as a fully realised individual art-direction project.

The completed book was a collaborative group outcome. Alongside it, Kylie developed and preserved an individual storyboard that explored a more abstract and interpretive narrative direction. The two directions were produced during the same project period. The individual direction was discussed with the professor but was not developed into the final photographed outcome.

Both belong to the project history, but they must never be conflated.

### Evidence and reflection boundary

This dossier separates three kinds of material:

1. **Contemporaneous project evidence**  
   The course brief, group presentation, research material, storyboards, photographic production, and final book.

2. **Confirmed authorial recollection**  
   Kylie's account of her contribution, the simultaneous development of the two narrative directions, the collaborative compromises involved, the origin of the title, and the professor feedback received on her individual storyboard.

3. **Later reflection**  
   Kylie's present interpretation of the project, including her preference for more open-ended visual language, her understanding of medium as a tool rather than the project's essence, and the project's place in the development of her creative practice.

Later reflection may explain the continuing value of the project. It must not be rewritten as if it were the group's original shared intention.

## 02. Executive Summary

**Madam, in Eden, I'm Adam** began with a tightly defined course constraint: retell Adam and Eve using photographs of hands alone.

The group moved beyond literal biblical reconstruction and connected the inherited narrative to contemporary questions of gender, power, marriage, possession, labour, control, freedom, and identity. This direction was developed into a staged photographic sequence and a final book.

During the same project period, Kylie also developed a separate storyboard. Rather than translating concepts into immediately recognisable objects, it broke the narrative into more abstract conditions: creation, rights, order, sin, punishment, silence, expulsion, destruction, and rebirth. Its imagery relied on fragments, darkness, material boundaries, gesture, light, and spatial relationships. This direction was shown to the professor and received feedback, but it was not selected for production.

The project therefore produced two distinct outcomes:

1. a completed collaborative photographic book using a more explicit symbolic language;
2. an unproduced individual storyboard documenting a more interpretive narrative strategy.

The value of the project today lies in the relationship between these outcomes. It preserves an early and honest record of collaboration, compromise, authorship, and narrative judgment. It also shows that Kylie's interest in perspective, interpretation, and the construction of meaning was already present at the beginning of her postgraduate practice, before those tendencies became more explicit in later projects.

## 04. The Course Brief

### The assigned problem

The brief asked the group to retell the story of Adam and Eve through close-up photography of hands.

The constraint removed the most familiar narrative devices:

- no faces;
- no full bodies;
- no conventional character acting;
- no direct recreation of Eden;
- no dependence on spoken or written dialogue.

The project therefore required narrative meaning to be carried through:

- gesture;
- touch;
- posture;
- objects;
- framing;
- light;
- sequence;
- editorial pacing.

### Required components

The documented brief included:

- research into the biblical story;
- research into depictions of Adam and Eve in art history;
- moodboard and storyboard development;
- a minimum photographic sequence;
- the design and production of a final photographic book.

### Why the constraint mattered

The hands-only rule was not merely a photographic style. It made relationships visible through small physical actions. A hand could offer, claim, conceal, command, submit, resist, protect, or release. Meaning had to emerge from the relationship between gestures, objects, and sequence rather than from facial expression or explanatory text.

This established the central formal condition of the project:

> The body would be represented through fragments, and the story would be built through relationships between those fragments.

## 05. Research Foundation

### Biblical narrative

The research began with Genesis and the established sequence of creation, prohibition, temptation, transgression, punishment, and expulsion.

Rather than treating the text as a neutral story, the research examined how authority and identity are organised within it:

- the order in which man and woman are created;
- the naming and governing authority assigned to man;
- the description of woman as derived from man and positioned as a helper;
- the distribution of blame;
- the consequences assigned to Eve;
- the continuation of gendered power after the expulsion.

### Art-historical context

The course required attention to historical depictions of Adam and Eve. This provided a wider visual context for understanding how the story had been repeatedly represented, interpreted, and inherited.

The project did not attempt to reproduce one historical image. The art-historical research established that the story had already been mediated by centuries of visual and cultural interpretation.

### The cultural construction of Eve

Kylie's research identified recurring descriptions attached to Eve in Western cultural and theological narratives, including ideas of disobedience, weakness, deception, seduction, moral failure, and untrustworthiness.

The research asked how these descriptions had become naturalised and how one narrated figure could carry a disproportionate symbolic burden across time.

This moved the inquiry away from the narrow question:

> Was Eve right or wrong?

and towards a more structural question:

> How has the narrative of Eve been constructed, repeated, and used to shape perceptions of women?

### Contemporary gender context

The group connected the inherited story to contemporary gender inequality and power relations.

The presentation referred to real social conditions in which women's autonomy could be restricted within marriage and domestic life, including control over technology, communication, and household responsibility.

This created the first major translation:

```text
Adam and Eve
      ↓
Marriage
      ↓
Power Relations
      ↓
Gender Inequality
      ↓
Contemporary Society
```

The project was therefore not a literal adaptation of Genesis. It used the inherited narrative as a lens through which to examine how power and gender continue to be organised and communicated.

## 06. Narrative Analysis

### From subject to structure

The research did not stop at collecting information about Adam, Eve, religion, or gender. It began to examine the structure through which meaning had been produced.

Key questions included:

- Who is granted authority within the story?
- Who is permitted to name, govern, speak, or interpret?
- How is responsibility distributed?
- Which qualities are repeatedly attached to Eve?
- Which actions by Adam receive less cultural attention?
- How does repetition turn interpretation into apparent truth?
- How might the same source material produce a different understanding when viewed from another perspective?

### Narrative bias

The project recognised that inherited narratives are not passive containers of information. They create frames through which characters, identities, and social roles are understood.

By isolating the construction of Eve, the research exposed how a story may continue to influence perception long after its original religious context.

This is the project's most important conceptual shift:

> The subject is not only Eve. The subject is the perspective through which Eve has been made legible.

### Reinterpretation rather than correction

The project did not attempt to produce a definitive new verdict on Genesis. Its stronger contribution was to create room for another reading.

The research notes challenged inherited assumptions by reconsidering actions and roles within the story. Eve could be read not only as disobedient, but also as active, curious, sharing, or transformative. Adam could be read not only as passive victim, but also through his silence, acceptance, or lack of intervention.

This did not resolve the story. It demonstrated that meaning changes when the frame changes.

## 07. Two Narrative Directions

During development, the project generated two simultaneous approaches. They shared the same brief and research context but organised meaning differently.

### Direction A: collaborative explicit symbolism

The group-developed direction translated themes into recognisable contemporary objects and actions.

Examples included visual associations such as:

- ring and marriage;
- gloves and domestic labour;
- phone and control or restricted autonomy;
- alcohol and conflict;
- grasping, pointing, holding, or withdrawing as signs of power and response.

This approach prioritised legibility. The viewer could identify the social issue quickly because the symbolic connection was direct.

Its strength was communicative immediacy. Its limitation was that interpretation could close too quickly: once the object had been decoded, little uncertainty remained.

### Direction B: Kylie's alternative storyboard

Kylie's individual storyboard organised the narrative through abstract conceptual stages, including:

- creation;
- order and rights;
- the construction of woman;
- temptation or disruption;
- original sin;
- punishment;
- silence;
- expulsion;
- destruction;
- rebirth.

Instead of matching each concept to a literal object, the imagery relied on:

- hands emerging through openings or cracks;
- layered or overlapping gestures;
- partial concealment;
- translucent barriers;
- darkness and void-like spaces;
- compressed or restrained forms;
- light, shadow, dust, and reappearance.

This approach did not tell the viewer exactly what each image meant. It created a visual structure in which interpretation would be slower and more participatory.

### Historical status of the two directions

The two directions must be represented accurately:

- both were developed during the original project period;
- the collaborative direction was selected and produced;
- the alternative storyboard was Kylie's individual exploration;
- the alternative storyboard was discussed with the professor and received feedback;
- it was not photographed or published as the final group outcome;
- Kylie did not regard the collaborative outcome as careless or invalid;
- the team's decisions reflected the realities and compromises of collaborative work.

The archive therefore records divergence, not a simple hierarchy of success and failure.

## 08. Team Development

### Collaboration

The project was completed by an international group during Kylie's first semester at NABA. Kylie was the only Chinese member of the class group and has described herself at that time as more reserved and less confident in insisting on her individual direction.

This context belongs to the authorial recollection of the process. It should not be used to diminish the team's contribution or imply that the final result was imposed without collaboration.

The final direction was developed and realised collectively. Kylie participated seriously in that process and does not describe the outcome as a regret. The project demonstrates both individual judgment and the ability to contribute within a shared production.

### From concept to shoot

The group translated its narrative into a sequence that could be staged with hands, objects, lighting, and controlled composition.

The production required:

- converting themes into shootable scenes;
- organising gesture and object relationships;
- maintaining visual continuity without faces or full bodies;
- coordinating the performance of hands;
- photographing the sequence;
- selecting and editing images;
- applying colour treatment;
- arranging the images into a coherent book.

### Kylie's participation

Within the collaborative outcome, Kylie contributed to the thinking and making of the project. Her role included visual and narrative development, shoot execution, hand performance, image treatment, and post-production.

Public descriptions should avoid expanding these contributions into unverified sole ownership of photography, art direction, or editorial design.

## 09. The Final Photographic Book

### Outcome

The final outcome was a collaborative photographic book constructed from staged images of hands.

The sequence translated the Adam and Eve narrative into contemporary relationships of:

- intimacy;
- union;
- possession;
- authority;
- domestic labour;
- conflict;
- technological control;
- resistance;
- separation;
- autonomy.

### Narrative logic

The book used repetition and progression to build a story without faces or conventional characters.

Hands changed function across the sequence. They could appear as:

- invitation;
- partnership;
- command;
- dependency;
- labour;
- pressure;
- refusal;
- escape.

Objects helped anchor the contemporary reading. They made social roles and conflicts immediately legible and allowed the group to connect an inherited myth to present-day power relations.

### Editorial function

The book format was not a neutral container. Sequence determined how each image changed the meaning of the next.

The editorial structure allowed the project to move from relationship and union towards control, tension, resistance, and separation. Meaning accumulated across pages rather than residing in a single photograph.

### Current critical assessment

The final book remains the completed historical outcome and should be preserved as such.

Kylie's present assessment is that some of its symbols are more illustrative than interpretive. Objects such as a ring, household gloves, or a phone can communicate quickly, but they may leave limited room for the viewer to construct a personal reading.

This is a later reflection, not evidence that the group failed to meet the original brief. The directness of the symbolism was also part of what made the narrative accessible and producible within the course context.

## 10. The Alternative Storyboard

### Status

The alternative storyboard is a contemporaneous individual project artefact. It is not a proposal retroactively invented for the portfolio, and it is not the final group outcome.

### Function

The storyboard demonstrates a different approach to the same source material.

Where the final book often used an object to establish a recognisable meaning, the alternative direction used spatial and material relationships to delay meaning:

```text
explicit symbol
      ↓
rapid recognition
      ↓
defined interpretation
```

versus:

```text
fragmented image
      ↓
uncertainty
      ↓
viewer interpretation
      ↓
constructed meaning
```

### Why it belongs in the dossier

The storyboard is important because it preserves thinking that did not become the final deliverable.

It proves that the project generated more than one valid response and that Kylie's preference for ambiguity, abstraction, and interpretive participation was already present during the original project.

It may be shown publicly as:

- **Alternative Narrative Direction**;
- **Independent Storyboard Exploration**;
- **Parallel Concept Development**.

It must not be captioned as the storyboard of the final photographic book unless a specific page directly corresponds to a produced sequence.

## 12. Communication and Design Principles

The following principles can be derived from the project evidence and current reflection.

### 12.1 Constraint can produce narrative invention

The hands-only rule limited representation but expanded the role of gesture, sequence, and relationship.

### 12.2 Images acquire meaning through sequence

No photograph operates independently. Editorial order changes the perceived action, emotional direction, and power relation of each image.

### 12.3 A symbol can communicate and over-determine

Recognisable objects help audiences understand a narrative quickly. The same clarity can also reduce interpretive space.

### 12.4 Perspective changes the reality a narrative produces

Re-reading Eve does not alter the source text. It changes which relationships, assumptions, and consequences become visible.

### 12.5 The medium serves the core inquiry

Photography is the project's realised medium, not its complete identity. The research could be translated through another medium if that medium served the central inquiry more effectively.

### 12.6 Collaboration and authorship can coexist

The final group outcome and Kylie's individual storyboard are not competing ownership claims. Together they document how shared production and personal judgment can diverge within the same project.

## 14. Later Reflection

> **Status:** The following section records Kylie's later understanding of the project. It does not describe the group's original shared project statement.

### Perspective, not medium

When asked what the project is fundamentally about, Kylie identified **perspective** or **mindset**, rather than photography, Genesis, or gender alone.

Genesis is the source case. Gender is a critical lens. Photography is the realised language. The deeper inquiry is how a perspective shapes what can be seen, believed, and understood.

### The medium is negotiable

Kylie describes images and visual form as her language, tools, and means of communication, but not as the essence of the work.

The core content remains primary. If another medium could communicate that core more effectively or make it more accessible, the medium could change.

This can be summarised as:

> The medium is negotiable. The perspective must remain coherent.

### Illustration and interpretation

Looking back, Kylie distinguishes between two narrative strategies:

- **illustrative communication**, which makes a position quickly legible;
- **interpretive communication**, which leaves space for the audience to participate in constructing meaning.

The collaborative final book largely demonstrates the former. The alternative storyboard points towards the latter.

Kylie now has greater confidence in preferring the interpretive direction, but this judgment does not invalidate the team's completed work or suggest that the alternative was historically more developed than it was.

### Ambiguity as participation

The project contributed to a later understanding:

> Ambiguity is not always a communication failure. It can be an invitation to participate.

This principle should not be applied without limit. Communication still requires enough structure for an audience to enter the work. The aim is not opacity. It is to avoid closing interpretation before the viewer has had a reason to engage.

### What the project changed

The project did not teach Kylie to become a photographer. It helped reveal a recurring authorial tendency:

- begin with the frame through which a subject is understood;
- question inherited categories;
- reconstruct narrative relationships;
- select a medium only after the conceptual core becomes clear;
- invite the audience to take part in meaning rather than merely receive a conclusion.

>
> The project produced a collaborative photographic book using recognisable objects, gestures, and editorial sequence to communicate its themes. During the same period, Kylie developed an individual alternative storyboard that translated the research through more abstract relationships of fragmentation, concealment, punishment, silence, and rebirth. The storyboard was discussed with the professor but remained unproduced.
>
> The distinction between these two directions is central to the project's current value. One demonstrates collective production and direct symbolic communication; the other preserves an early inclination towards ambiguity, metaphor, and viewer participation. Presented together and credited accurately, they form an honest record of research, collaboration, authorship, and the development of Kylie's narrative methodology.

## 24. Closing Reflection

This project should not be preserved because every part of it represents Kylie's current visual preference.

It should be preserved because it records a formative moment with unusual clarity.

The brief created a constraint. Research exposed the cultural work performed by an inherited story. Collaboration turned that inquiry into a completed book. A parallel storyboard preserved a different authorial instinct. Later reflection made it possible to understand the distance between those directions without erasing either one.

The final book proves that the project was completed.

The alternative storyboard proves that another way of seeing was already present.

The reflection proves that Kylie can return to an early work, distinguish evidence from interpretation, respect collaboration, and still articulate what the project changed in her practice.

That combination - realised outcome, unproduced thinking, and critical reconstruction - is the project's most complete legacy.
