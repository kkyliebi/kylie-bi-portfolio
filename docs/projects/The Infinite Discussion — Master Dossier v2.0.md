## 1. Project Definition

*The Infinite Discussion* is a speculative audiovisual installation that began with a brief about machine vision and developed into an investigation of machine perspective, communication, and open-ended exchange.

The final work presents two computers engaged in a continuing recorded discussion. Rather than illustrating what machines can see, the installation asks what “vision” means when it is understood as a way of interpreting the world. The computers do not function as neutral display devices. They become participants within a constructed situation: two machine positions placed in relation, each encountering and responding to a different point of view.

The project is not a demonstration of artificial intelligence, an interface prototype, or a prediction of autonomous machine consciousness. Its central contribution is conceptual and communicative. It reframes a technology-centred brief by asking a more fundamental question:

> If vision is more than optical capture, what kind of perspective might a machine possess?

From that question, the project moves toward another:

> What happens when two machine perspectives are placed in conditions where neither is required to reach a final answer?

The installation is the material form of that enquiry.

## 3. Executive Summary

The project originated in an academic brief concerning machine vision. Kylie’s first response was a dashcam-based proposal connected to a public communication context in Milan. The proposal treated the camera as a machine eye: a device that records an event and could potentially participate in a communication intervention.

The proposal was rejected as too shallow. The decisive issue was not simply that the idea required refinement. It was that it remained at the most literal interpretation of the brief:

```text
Machine
→ Camera
→ Seeing
→ Recorded image
```

The rejection caused the project’s central question to change. Instead of asking what a machine can record, Kylie began asking what “vision” actually means. Vision could no longer be treated only as sight. It could also mean position, interpretation, judgement, or a way of understanding the world.

This reframing moved the project from **machine eye** to **machine perspective**.

The concept then developed through several spatial and narrative forms. Early directions imagined a conference room, talking furniture or objects, multiple machines, and an enclosed display environment. These proposals explored how different non-human positions might be staged, but they also introduced unnecessary narrative and production complexity.

Through iteration, the system was reduced to its essential relationship:

```text
Two computers
→ Two positions
→ One shared subject
→ An exchange without required resolution
```

The working frame of a **debate** was subsequently rejected. Debate implied opposition, performance, and an outcome: one side wins, persuades, or defeats the other. That structure contradicted the project’s emerging interest in continued communication between different perspectives. The title and format therefore shifted to *The Infinite Discussion*.

The installation was initially conceived as a real-time exchange. Technical and network limitations made that form unreliable in the exhibition context. Rather than treating the constraint as a hidden failure, the project changed its implementation: the discussion was generated and assembled in advance, then presented as a pre-rendered audiovisual sequence.

This decision preserved the conceptual experience that mattered—two machines appearing to occupy an ongoing communicative relationship—while ensuring that timing, continuity, subtitles, sound, and exhibition playback remained controlled.

The final project therefore represents more than a completed installation. It records a sequence of reframings:

```text
Camera
→ Vision
→ Perspective
→ Multiple perspectives
→ Discussion
→ Conditions for continued exchange
```

## 4. Historical Context

### 4.1 The Brief: Machine Vision

The project began from a brief centred on **machine vision**. The phrase initially appeared to direct attention toward technologies of sight: cameras, sensors, recognition systems, and machine-readable images.

The historical importance of the brief lies in its ambiguity. “Vision” can describe optical perception, but it can also describe a worldview, an interpretation, an ambition, or a position from which something is understood.

The project did not begin with this expanded definition already resolved. That meaning emerged because the first response failed to move beyond the literal reading of the brief.

### 4.2 The Dashcam Proposal

The first proposal used the dashcam as an example of a machine that sees. It connected machine recording to a public communication context in Milan and explored whether a camera-based system could become part of an intervention.

Historically, this proposal matters because it establishes the starting point of the project:

- the machine was understood through a familiar visual device;
- vision was equated with recording;
- the project concentrated on what the device captured;
- the communication proposition remained attached to the image produced by the machine.

The dashcam direction should not be erased from the project history. It is the evidence that the final concept was not an immediate stylistic invention. It grew from a rejected interpretation.

### 4.3 Tutor Rejection

The tutor rejected the dashcam proposal as shallow.

The rejection did not simply ask for a more polished version of the same idea. It exposed a problem in the framing. The concept still treated machine vision as a technical capability rather than a conceptual question.

The productive consequence was:

> The project stopped trying to answer the brief and began examining the meaning of the brief itself.

This is the first major turning point.

---

## 5. Reframing “Vision”

After the rejection, Kylie began to interrogate the word **vision**.

If vision is only visual capture, then a dashcam is an adequate example. But if vision also means a way of interpreting reality, a camera cannot fully answer the question. It records from a position, but it does not by itself explain the position represented.

The enquiry therefore moved through the following distinctions:

| Initial reading | Reframed reading |
|---|---|
| Vision as sight | Vision as perspective |
| Machine as camera | Machine as situated interpreter |
| Image as evidence | Position as a condition of meaning |
| Seeing an event | Understanding differently |
| Technical output | Communicative relationship |

This reframing did not claim that machines possess human-like inner experience. The project used speculation to make differences in perspective visible and discussable.

The question was no longer:

> What does a machine see?

It became:

> From what position might a machine interpret?

And then:

> What happens when different machine positions meet?

This shift generated the project’s subsequent narrative and spatial development.

## 6. Conceptual Evolution

The final installation emerged through progressive reduction. Each stage tested a different way of materialising machine perspective.

### 6.1 Conference Room

An early concept placed the exchange in a conference-room setting.

The conference room gave the machines a recognisable social structure. It suggested meeting, negotiation, hierarchy, turn-taking, and organised discussion. It helped translate an abstract question—how machine perspectives might encounter one another—into a spatial scene.

At the same time, the setting risked making the project too theatrical and too dependent on human institutional conventions. The room could become the story, when the relationship between perspectives needed to remain the subject.

### 6.2 Talking Furniture and Objects

The concept expanded to furniture or objects that could speak.

This direction tested whether non-human perspectives could be distributed across everyday things. Different objects could occupy different roles, histories, or positions. The environment itself might become conversational.

The proposal opened the system, but it also multiplied variables:

- each object required a reason to speak;
- characterisation risked replacing conceptual precision;
- the audience might focus on anthropomorphic personalities;
- multiple voices made the core relationship harder to read;
- production complexity increased without necessarily deepening the question.

The value of this stage was not the final form. It revealed that the project did not need an entire fictional world. It needed a clear encounter between positions.

### 6.3 Multiple Machines

The project then moved from talking objects toward machines as participants.

This clarified the relationship to the original brief. The participants would no longer be metaphorical objects representing points of view; they would be recognisable computing devices placed into a communicative situation.

However, a larger group still created noise. The more participants the system contained, the more the installation required management, explanation, and narrative differentiation.

### 6.4 Enclosed Display Environment

An enclosed transparent structure—described during development as a glass or acrylic box—was explored as a way to stage and contain the machine exchange.

The enclosure introduced several useful associations:

- experiment;
- observation;
- museum display;
- specimen;
- controlled environment;
- separation between participants and audience.

It positioned viewers as observers of a system already in operation.

The display logic remained relevant to the installation’s exhibition character, but the project continued to simplify the number of elements inside it.

### 6.5 Headphones and Individual Listening

Headphones were considered as a way to provide access to the machines’ voices without allowing sound to dissolve into the room.

This decision acknowledged that the discussion required close listening. It also created a distinction between seeing the installation and entering its communicative layer. The audience could first encounter two computers as objects, then access the exchange through sound and text.

Whether headphones remained in the exact final installation configuration should be verified against final documentation before publication. Their conceptual role in the development is supported: they were part of the attempt to make a machine-to-machine exchange perceptible to a human observer.

### 6.6 Two Computers

The concept was ultimately reduced to two computers.

This was not merely a production simplification. It clarified the project’s fundamental unit:

> Perspective becomes meaningful through relation to another perspective.

With two participants, difference could exist without becoming a crowd. The exchange could remain legible. The computers could face, address, or answer one another while the audience observed a relationship rather than navigating a catalogue of characters.

The final system was therefore deliberately minimal:

```text
Computer A
↕
Shared subject
↕
Computer B
```

The two machines created enough difference for communication to occur and enough containment for that communication to be understood.

## 7. From Debate to Discussion

### 7.1 Why Debate Was Initially Useful

The project initially used **debate** as the structure for the exchange.

Debate offered:

- two recognisable positions;
- alternation between voices;
- a clear communicative rhythm;
- disagreement as visible content;
- a familiar frame through which an audience could understand machine dialogue.

As a working device, it helped transform “different machine perspectives” into a scriptable form.

### 7.2 Why Debate Became Insufficient

Debate also carried assumptions that increasingly conflicted with the project:

- positions are organised as opponents;
- each contribution serves an argumentative strategy;
- the exchange is directed toward persuasion;
- success is measured through victory, defeat, or resolution;
- disagreement is treated as something to settle.

The project was becoming interested in a different condition: perspectives remaining in contact without being forced into agreement.

The question was not which machine was correct. Nor was the purpose to stage a spectacular conflict between two artificial personalities. The more relevant enquiry was whether communication could continue when different interpretations remained unresolved.

### 7.3 The Infinite Discussion

The shift from **debate** to **discussion** changed both the title and the conceptual structure.

Discussion allowed:

- difference without declared opposition;
- response without conquest;
- continuation without conclusion;
- ambiguity without failure;
- exchange as an ongoing condition.

“Infinite” does not need to be interpreted as a literal technical claim that the system generated language forever. It describes the conceptual condition of the work: no final authority closes the exchange.

The title therefore names the project’s central proposition:

> Communication can remain meaningful without producing a single final answer.

This was a historical design decision, not a later portfolio slogan.

## 8. The Communication Framework

The project’s communication system can be understood through five components.

### 8.1 Participants

Two computers occupy distinct positions within the installation.

They are treated as communicative participants, not merely screens used to display human-authored content.

### 8.2 Shared Subject

The machines require a subject around which different positions can form.

The subject is less important than the structure it enables. The project is not primarily a claim about the chosen topic; it is an investigation into how multiple perspectives encounter one another.

The exact discussion topic or topics should be taken from final scripts before any public description names them.

### 8.3 Difference

The exchange depends on non-identical positions. If both machines simply repeat the same account, discussion collapses into confirmation.

Difference is therefore not a flaw in the system. It is the condition that makes the system communicative.

### 8.4 Sequence

Speech, reply, timing, pause, sound, and subtitles make the exchange legible.

The discussion is experienced through duration. Meaning does not belong to one isolated statement; it develops through the relationship between contributions.

### 8.5 Observation

The audience does not need to control the exchange for the work to function.

The viewer’s role is to encounter a communication system in progress: to observe positions forming, responding, diverging, and continuing.

This is why the final outcome is more accurately described as an experimental communication environment than as a conventional interactive interface.

## 9. Design Method

The project did not follow a fixed methodology declared in advance. Its method became visible through the work.

### 9.1 Observe the Given Frame

The starting point was the phrase “machine vision” and its most immediate technical associations.

### 9.2 Identify the Limitation

The dashcam proposal revealed that optical capture alone could not sustain the intended enquiry.

### 9.3 Reframe the Question

Vision was reinterpreted as perspective.

### 9.4 Construct a Context

Conference rooms, objects, multiple machines, enclosures, and listening devices were tested as possible conditions in which perspectives could meet.

### 9.5 Remove What Distracts

Characters, objects, participants, and spatial elements were reduced until the central relationship became legible.

### 9.6 Establish the Conditions

Two computers, distinct positions, a shared subject, timed audiovisual exchange, and an observing audience formed the final system.

### 9.7 Preserve the Experience Under Constraint

When real-time operation proved unreliable, the implementation changed while the central communicative condition was retained.

Looking back, this sequence can be summarised as:

```text
Observe
→ Reframe
→ Construct context
→ Establish conditions
→ Observe what emerges
```

This formulation is a retrospective articulation of the project’s actual development, not a claim that the project began with a named five-step methodology.

## 10. Real-Time Intention

The installation was initially intended to operate in real time.

Real-time exchange was conceptually attractive because it would make the discussion responsive, unpredictable, and potentially continuous. The machines would not merely replay a completed conversation; they would appear to produce the relationship as the audience encountered it.

The initial ambition can be described as:

```text
Prompt or shared subject
→ Machine position A
→ Machine position B
→ Continuing exchange
→ Live audiovisual output
```

This intention aligned with the title’s interest in continuation. It also introduced substantial practical dependencies:

- stable network access;
- reliable generation time;
- consistent software behaviour;
- synchronisation between two outputs;
- control of speech, subtitles, and pacing;
- resilience throughout an exhibition period.

The project could not assume those conditions would remain stable.

## 11. Real-Time to Pre-Rendered

### 11.1 The Constraint

Network and implementation limitations made the planned real-time system unreliable for exhibition.

This did not invalidate the concept, but it created a design decision:

1. preserve the claim of live autonomy and accept a fragile experience; or
2. preserve the communicative experience by changing how it was produced.

The second option was chosen.

### 11.2 The Decision

The machine discussion was generated and prepared in advance, then edited into a controlled audiovisual sequence for playback.

This allowed the project to manage:

- continuity;
- duration;
- turn-taking;
- voice;
- subtitles;
- sound-image synchronisation;
- technical reliability;
- the audience’s ability to follow the exchange.

### 11.3 What Changed

The final work did not demonstrate a live autonomous system.

Any public description must avoid claiming:

- real-time audience interaction;
- live machine-to-machine generation;
- autonomous conversation during the exhibition;
- adaptive responses to visitors.

### 11.4 What Remained

The conceptual core remained:

- two machine positions;
- a continuing exchange;
- disagreement without required victory;
- the audience observing a communicative relationship;
- an installation organised around duration rather than a single image.

The implementation decision should therefore be understood as a transparent adaptation, not disguised as technical completion.

## 12. Audiovisual and Spatial Decisions

### 12.1 Two Voices

Distinct voices helped separate the two positions and made turn-taking audible.

Development notes refer to differentiated voices, including a male/female distinction. This should be described publicly as a legibility and sequencing decision unless the original scripts demonstrate that gender itself was an explicit subject of the work. The dossier does not infer symbolic meaning from that choice.

### 12.2 Subtitles

Subtitles made the exchange readable and supported comprehension within an exhibition environment.

They also reinforced the project’s hybrid nature: the discussion existed simultaneously as voice, text, timing, and screen-based image.

### 12.3 Screens as Bodies

The computers were not neutral playback equipment. Their physical presence gave the exchange spatial form.

The audience encountered:

- two separate objects;
- two sources of voice and text;
- an interval between them;
- a relationship made visible through placement.

The space between the machines became part of the communication system.

### 12.4 Enclosure and Observation

The explored glass or acrylic enclosure framed the system as something to observe. It introduced the language of experiment and exhibition without requiring the audience to become an operator.

Final claims about material, dimensions, and exact installation configuration must follow the final photographic or technical record.

### 12.5 Duration

The project depended on sequence rather than instant comprehension.

The visitor needed time to recognise:

1. that two machines were positioned in relation;
2. that their voices represented different perspectives;
3. that neither perspective produced a definitive closure;
4. that continuation itself was the project’s communicative condition.

## 13. Final Outcome

The final outcome was a pre-rendered audiovisual installation centred on two computers engaged in a structured, continuing discussion.

The verified conceptual components are:

- two computers as communicative participants;
- differentiated positions or voices;
- a shared discussion structure;
- spoken audiovisual material;
- subtitles;
- an exhibition-oriented spatial arrangement;
- a pre-rendered rather than live implementation;
- an unresolved exchange represented as ongoing.

The outcome should not be described as a functioning AI dialogue product. It is a speculative installation that uses machine dialogue to investigate perspective and communication.

## 15. Later Reflection

Looking back, *The Infinite Discussion* is significant because it marks a change in how Kylie approaches a brief.

The project did not progress by finding a better visual answer to “machine vision.” It progressed by questioning whether the brief’s apparent subject had been understood correctly.

The decisive move was:

```text
Do not optimise the first answer.
Reconsider the frame that produced the answer.
```

This project therefore reveals several patterns that later became more visible in Kylie’s practice.

### 15.1 Context Before Artefact

The final form only became possible after the context changed.

The computers, voices, subtitles, and installation were not selected as a preferred visual style. They emerged from a new context: two machine perspectives placed in conditions where exchange could continue.

### 15.2 Conditions Rather Than Conclusions

Kylie did not script the project solely to deliver a predetermined answer about AI.

The more important design task was to establish:

- who or what could participate;
- how difference would remain legible;
- what form of exchange would be possible;
- what conditions would prevent premature closure;
- how an audience could observe the result.

### 15.3 Reframing as Design Work

The rejection of the dashcam proposal became productive because it changed the question.

The project demonstrates that reframing is not preliminary explanation before design begins. Reframing is itself a design act: it changes what can be made.

### 15.4 Emergence

In retrospect, the project shows an early interest in observing what a constructed situation produces rather than determining every meaning in advance.

This should be described carefully. The final installation was pre-rendered, so its technical behaviour was not emergent in real time. The emergent quality belongs to the conceptual and editorial process: the project was interested in what becomes visible when distinct positions are allowed to continue in relation.

### 15.5 Technology as Medium, Not Subject

The work uses computers, machine-generated dialogue, voice, and screens, but its lasting question is not whether the technology is impressive.

The technology provides a medium through which perspective, difference, and communication can be examined.

## 16. What the Project Does Not Claim

To preserve historical and professional accuracy, the project does not claim:

- that the final installation ran as a live autonomous conversation;
- that visitors directly controlled or prompted the machines;
- that the system adapted to audience behaviour;
- that the work proves machines possess consciousness or emotion;
- that the two computers independently formed philosophical positions during exhibition;
- that the project produced scientific findings about machine cognition;
- that the installation resolved the ethics or future of artificial intelligence;
- that every portfolio-wide methodological insight was explicitly articulated during the original project.

These boundaries strengthen the project. Its value lies in the precision of the question and the construction of a communicative situation, not in inflated technical claims.

## 20. Communication Principles Discovered

The following principles are grounded in the project’s development:

### 20.1 A Brief Can Contain the Wrong Question

The first responsibility is not always to answer what appears to have been asked. It may be to determine what the question actually means.

### 20.2 Vision Is Situated

Seeing is not neutral. Perspective is shaped by position, relation, and interpretation.

### 20.3 Difference Enables Discussion

Communication does not require participants to collapse into one view.

### 20.4 Continuation Can Be an Outcome

An exchange does not fail merely because it does not reach agreement.

### 20.5 Simplicity Can Clarify a System

Reducing conference rooms, objects, and multiple machines to two computers made the central relationship more legible.

### 20.6 Implementation Must Serve the Concept

When live operation became unreliable, the project changed its technical form while retaining its communicative structure.

### 20.7 Speculation Requires Honest Boundaries

The installation can invite audiences to consider machine perspective without claiming that the machines possess human consciousness or that the final playback was autonomous.

## 27. Canonical Project Statement

> *The Infinite Discussion* is a speculative audiovisual installation developed from an academic brief about machine vision. The project began with a dashcam proposal that treated the camera as a machine eye. After that direction was rejected as too literal, the enquiry shifted from optical capture to perspective: not simply what a machine sees, but from what position it might interpret.
>
> The concept evolved through conference rooms, talking objects, multiple machines, and an enclosed observation environment before being reduced to two computers engaged in a continuing exchange. An early debate structure was abandoned because it required opposition and resolution. Discussion offered a more appropriate condition: different perspectives could remain in contact without one becoming final.
>
> The installation was initially conceived as a real-time system. When network and technical constraints made live operation unreliable, the dialogue was generated and assembled in advance as a controlled audiovisual sequence. This changed the implementation but preserved the project’s central relationship.
>
> The final work does not claim autonomous machine consciousness or live audience interaction. It uses the fiction of machine discussion to investigate how perspective is constructed, how difference becomes communicative, and how a designed context can allow an exchange to continue without forcing closure.

## 28. Final Editorial Position

*The Infinite Discussion* should be presented as a project about **reframing, perspective, and the conditions of communication**.

It should not be reduced to:

- an AI experiment;
- a machine-learning demonstration;
- a screen installation;
- a debate between computers;
- an immersive technology showcase.

Its strongest and most accurate narrative is chronological:

```text
Rejected dashcam proposal
→ Reconsidering “vision”
→ Vision as perspective
→ Staging different perspectives
→ Simplifying the context
→ Two computers
→ Debate becomes discussion
→ Real-time becomes pre-rendered
→ An unresolved audiovisual exchange
```

This history is the project’s conceptual evidence. The final installation matters because it materialises that sequence of decisions—not because it performs technological novelty.
